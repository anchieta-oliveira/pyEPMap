# Summary

Electrostatic Potential Maps (EPM) represent the charge distribution in molecules, allowing the distribution of charges to be visualized. Knowledge of charge distributions is an important tool in structural biology and can be used to understand how molecules interact and other structural properties. So, we developed an application and package in Python to build 3D electrostatic maps of molecules, based on partial charges, parallelized on CPU (Numba) and multi-GPU (CuPy). The application makes it possible to calculate electrostatic maps in large molecular systems, such as biomolecules, with more than half a million atoms and a 3D grid with 16 million points efficiently. 

# Statement of need

The electrostatic potential $(ϕ)$ is a description of the electrostatic potential generated by a molecule, taking into account the distribution of electrostatic charges around its atoms. In biomolecular systems, charge distribution plays an essential role in the binding of ligands to proteins, interaction between nucleic acids and catalytic mechanisms in enzymes [@suresh2023molecular; @murray2011electrostatic; @Celik12102024]. In this way, the calculation of efficient electrostatic potential maps in large molecular systems aids the understanding of biological and chemical processes [@chowdhury2022efficient; @zhang2021efficient; @lopez2019efficient].
Classically, the electrostatic potential is obtained using the Coulomb equation, which expresses the influence of the point charges distributed in the molecule on a point in three-dimensional space:

$$\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) = \sum_{i} \frac{q_i}{\|\boldsymbol{r} - \boldsymbol{r}_i\|}$$

where $q_i$ represents the partial charge of the atom $i$, and $\|\boldsymbol{r} - \boldsymbol{r}_i\|$ the distance between the atom and the specific point. To construct the 3D electrostatic potential maps, a 3D grid is built around the molecule, where the Coulomb potential is calculated for each point on the grid. However, for large systems, the computational cost can become prohibitive due to the need to evaluate the influence of each charge on millions of points on the grid [@STONE2023415].

To mitigate this limitation, we have developed an computational package that employs massive parallelization both on CPU, using Numba [@lam2024numba], and on GPU, using CuPy [@cupy_learningsys2017]. This approach allows electrostatic map calculations to be made feasible for systems containing hundreds of thousands of atoms and high-resolution grids.

# Example application

The electrostatic influence in the formation of nucleosomes is well known, where histones present positive charges in the region of interaction with DNA, complementing the negative phosphorus charges present in the phosphodiester bond of DNA [@shimizu2024asymmetric]. \autoref{fig:fig_8Y3C} shows the Electrostatic Potential Map (EPMap) calculated with (pyEPMap) isolated di-nucleosome protein (closed form) (20,416 atoms and 10,289,600 grid points) and complex DNA protein (36,273 atoms and 18,298,710 grid points) (PDB ID: 8Y3C) [@shimizu2024asymmetric]. Note the electrostatic complementarity between the positive charges of the protein (blue) and the negative charges of the RNA (red).

![Electrostatic potential maps (EPM) of the isolated di-nucleosome protein (closed form) (20,416 atoms and 10,289,600 grid points) on the left and the complex DNA protein (36,273 atoms and 18,298,710 grid points) on the right (PDB ID: 8Y3C). The electrostatic complementarity between the positive charges of the protein (blue) and the negative charges of the RNA (red). \label{fig:fig_8Y3C}](fig_8Y3C.png)

\autoref{fig:fig_1PGL} shows the electrostatic potential map (EPM) of the Bean Pod Mottle Virus (BPMV), whose three-dimensional structure was obtained from the PDB database (ID: 1PGL) [@lin2003evidence]. BPMV contains 525,600 atoms, and the map was generated on a grid composed of 16,836,644 points, demonstrating pyEPMap's ability to handle biomolecular systems with many atoms on a grid with high resolution (grid space of 1.0 A). Parallelization on multiple GPUs allows for significant acceleration of calculations, reducing the time needed to build the EPM.
